One possible feature is the imbalance between visible liquidity on the bid and ask sides of the order book.

```python
def order_book_imbalance(bids, asks):
    total_bid_volume = sum(size for price, size in bids)
    total_ask_volume = sum(size for price, size in asks)

    visible_volume = total_bid_volume + total_ask_volume

    if visible_volume == 0:
        return 0.0

    imbalance = (
        total_bid_volume - total_ask_volume
    ) / visible_volume

    return imbalance

bids = [
    (0.46, 1200),
    (0.45, 850),
    (0.44, 600),
]

asks = [
    (0.47, 700),
    (0.48, 540),
    (0.49, 390),
]

result = order_book_imbalance(bids, asks)

print(f"Order Book Imbalance: {result:.2%}")
```

This metric does not predict how the market will resolve.

Visible liquidity can disappear before execution, and large orders far away from the best bid or ask may never influence the market.

That is why order book imbalance is usually combined with movement in the underlying asset, recent trade flow, and time remaining.

A strong system does not try to trade every move. Most potential signals are rejected before the bot submits a single order.

## 2\. How a market event becomes fair value

A human may describe a sharp Bitcoin move as bullish or bearish. That is not precise enough for an algorithm.

The bot needs to estimate how much a specific signal should change the probability of Up in this exact market with this exact amount of time remaining.

Suppose Up is trading at **43¢**.

BTC moves above the opening price, aggressive buying appears on the external market, and stale sell orders remain available in the Polymarket CLOB.

The bot is not asking:

> **“Does Bitcoin look strong right now?”**

It is asking:

> **“What should the probability of Up be after this new information?”**

A **Bayesian** model can be used to update that estimate.

- **The updated probability of Up equals the probability of seeing the current signal when Up wins, multiplied by the previous probability of Up, divided by the combined weighted probability of seeing that signal under both Up and Down outcomes.**

```python
def bayesian_fair_value(
    prior_up,
    signal_probability_if_up,
    signal_probability_if_down,
):
    weighted_up = (
        prior_up
        * signal_probability_if_up
    )

    weighted_down = (
        (1 - prior_up)
        * signal_probability_if_down
    )

    posterior_up = (
        weighted_up
        / (weighted_up + weighted_down)
    )

    return posterior_up

prior_probability = 0.43

updated_probability = bayesian_fair_value(
    prior_up=prior_probability,
    signal_probability_if_up=0.66,
    signal_probability_if_down=0.39,
)

print(
    f"Previous Fair Value: "
    f"{prior_probability:.2%}"
)

print(
    f"Updated Fair Value: "
    f"{updated_probability:.2%}"
)
```

Suppose the model valued Up at 43% before the move and raises its fair value to approximately 56% after processing the signal.

If the market is still offering Up at 43¢, the theoretical difference is 13 percentage points.

That still does not mean the bot should immediately buy.

The model may be counting the same underlying event several times.

For example, a sharp BTC move may simultaneously create:

> \> higher trading volume > a sequence of aggressive market buys > stronger bid-side liquidity > similar movement in ETH and SOL

These appear to be four separate indicators, but they may all be consequences of the same initial move.

Treating them as independent confirmations would push the model toward an unrealistically high fair value.

A well-designed system must evaluate not only the strength of each signal, but also how much information it genuinely adds beyond the other features.

## 3\. Fair value and real PnL are not the same thing

Suppose the Pricing Model values Up at **56¢**, while the first available sell order is priced at **47¢**.

At first glance, that looks like a nine-percentage-point edge.

But the bot cannot treat the entire difference as profit.

It still needs to account for:

> \> trading fees > bid-ask spread > expected slippage > worse prices at larger size > partial-fill risk > uncertainty in the fair-value model

**Net Edge equals fair value minus the expected average fill price, trading fees, slippage, and an additional safety buffer.**

```python
def calculate_executable_edge(
    fair_value,
    expected_fill_price,
    trading_fee,
    expected_slippage,
    safety_buffer,
):
    gross_edge = (
        fair_value
        - expected_fill_price
    )

    net_edge = (
        gross_edge
        - trading_fee
        - expected_slippage
        - safety_buffer
    )

    return gross_edge, net_edge

gross, net = calculate_executable_edge(
    fair_value=0.56,
    expected_fill_price=0.47,
    trading_fee=0.012,
    expected_slippage=0.006,
    safety_buffer=0.010,
)

print(f"Gross Edge: {gross:.2%}")
print(f"Net Edge: {net:.2%}")
```

After these deductions, a raw nine-point gap may fall to only five or six points.

The bot must then inspect the depth of the order book.

The first 100 shares may be available at 47¢, the next 300 at 49¢, and the remaining size at 51¢.

That means the trade should be evaluated using its expected **volume-weighted average fill price**, not the best ask displayed at the top of the book.

The same principle applies to binary arbitrage.

**An arbitrage opportunity exists only when equal quantities of Up and Down can be fully acquired for less than $1 after all execution costs.**

For example:

> Up is available at 46¢ Down is available at 48¢ combined pair cost: 94¢ payout after resolution: $1

The theoretical margin is 6¢ per matched pair.

But if the required Down size actually fills at an average of 53¢, the real pair cost becomes 99¢ before fees.

A backtest may identify an attractive opportunity in a historical order-book snapshot.

A live bot has to prove that the displayed price was genuinely executable.

The model identifies potential value. Execution determines whether the bot can capture it.

## 4\. Why edge often appears between 5m and 15m markets

The same BTC move can affect several related markets at once:

> the current BTC Up or Down 5m market the next five-minute window BTC Up or Down 15m short-duration ETH and SOL markets markets with nearby resolution times

But these markets do not necessarily reprice at the same speed.

Each one has a different:

> opening price time remaining liquidity profile group of active participants distribution of resting limit orders probability of filling at the displayed price

Suppose BTC moves sharply higher.

The current 5m market quickly reprices Up from 51¢ to 72¢, while the 15m market is still trading near 55¢.

That does not automatically mean 15m Up is underpriced.

The 15m market has a different opening price and more time remaining before resolution.

The bot first calculates an independent fair value for each market and only then compares the size of their deviations.

A z-score can be used to identify an unusually wide relationship between connected markets.

**The z-score equals the current spread between two related markets minus its historical average, divided by the historical standard deviation of that spread.**

```python
def cross_market_zscore(
    current_spread,
    average_spread,
    spread_standard_deviation,
):
    if spread_standard_deviation <= 0:
        raise ValueError(
            "Standard deviation must be positive"
        )

    zscore = (
        current_spread - average_spread
    ) / spread_standard_deviation

    return zscore

z = cross_market_zscore(
    current_spread=0.091,
    average_spread=0.034,
    spread_standard_deviation=0.020,
)

print(f"Cross-Market Z-Score: {z:.2f}")
```

A high z-score indicates that the relationship has moved far outside its normal range.

The model then needs to determine why:

> \> one market may not have repriced yet > another market may have overreacted > liquidity may have disappeared in only one CLOB > different opening prices may fully justify the gap

The system tracks several connected fair values and reacts when one outcome price begins trading outside the structure implied by the rest.

## 5\. Five ways bots turn edge into PnL

Once a pricing discrepancy has been identified, the bot must choose not only which outcome to trade, but also how to structure the position.

Five recurring models appear across profitable systems.

**1️⃣** **Temporal Arbitrage**

Traditional binary arbitrage appears when equal quantities of Up and Down can be bought at the same time for less than $1.

Temporal Arbitrage builds the two sides at different moments.

Suppose BTC moves higher and Down falls to **29¢**. The bot gradually buys 500 Down shares.

Several minutes later, BTC moves back toward the opening price, and Up can now be accumulated at an average price of **51¢**.

The final position becomes:

> 500 Down at 29¢ 500 Up at 51¢ cost per matched pair: 80¢ payout after resolution: $1

The two prices were never available simultaneously.

The bot constructed the arbitrage across two different market states.

**Main risk:** the second outcome may never become cheap enough.

After buying Down, BTC could continue moving higher until the end of the window. The bot would then be left with unhedged Down inventory instead of a protected pair.

More conservative systems build the structure in smaller blocks: first 50 Down, then 50 Up, and only increase size once the first pair is complete.

**2️⃣** **Hedged Directional Position**

This model combines a protected base with a smaller directional imbalance.

Suppose the bot holds:

> \> 310 Up > 286 Down

Of these, 286 Up and 286 Down form matched inventory. The remaining 24 Up shares create directional exposure.

If the market resolves Up, the additional shares increase the payout. If Down wins, most of the position remains protected.

The size of the imbalance can change as fair value changes:

> stronger Up signal - add more Up weaker signal - reduce Up higher risk - add more Down no remaining edge - rebalance the position

**Main risk:** the matched inventory may be too expensive.

If the average Up price is 54¢ and the average Down price is 49¢, each protected pair costs $1.03 but can return only $1.

The directional excess must first recover that deficit before the complete position becomes profitable.

**3️⃣** **Inventory Market Making**

An inventory bot manages capital across several markets instead of treating every trade as an isolated position.

It may simultaneously trade:

> BTC Up or Down 5m BTC Up or Down 15m ETH Up or Down 5m SOL Up or Down 15m Up and Down across neighboring windows

For every market, the system monitors:

> average Up cost average Down cost the number of matched shares directional excess available collateral liquidity available for an exit

Suppose Down becomes the clear favorite and rises to **98¢**.

Instead of waiting for resolution, the bot may sell part of its Down inventory at 98¢, release capital immediately, and retain only the core position.

It may also use a small part of the released capital to buy Up at 2¢ as inexpensive protection against an extreme final-second move.

**Main risk:** directionally neutral inventory can still lose money.

If both outcomes were accumulated at a combined average cost above $1, the settlement payout cannot cover the cost of the matched position.

**4️⃣** **Near-Resolution Capture**

These bots enter shortly before the market resolves.

When the result appears almost certain, the winning outcome may still be available at **98–99¢**.

The bot buys the remaining liquidity and waits for the $1 payout.

Example:

> entry price: 98.8¢ resolution value: $1 gross profit: 1.2¢ per share

The return per trade is small, so the strategy depends on high turnover and an extremely accurate understanding of the market’s resolution mechanics.

**Main risk:** a rare loss is much larger than a normal win.

One incorrect purchase at 99¢ can erase the profit from dozens of successful trades earning 1¢ each.

Potential causes include:

> a final-second move in the underlying asset monitoring the wrong price feed using an incorrect opening price misunderstanding the resolution rules failing to cancel a limit order in time

A high win rate does not make this strategy low-risk.

**5️⃣** **Dynamic Position Rotation**

A rotation bot does not need to hold its original outcome until resolution.

Suppose the model initially considers Up underpriced in a BTC Up or Down 5m market and begins accumulating Up.

BTC then moves back toward the opening price, selling pressure increases, and the model lowers its fair value for Up.

The algorithm may:

> cancel its remaining Up buy orders sell part of the existing Up position begin accumulating Down rotate again if another strong signal appears

The goal is not to select the correct outcome once and remain committed to it.

The bot tries to maintain more exposure to whichever side currently offers the strongest net edge.

**Main risk:** excessive rotation.

In a noisy market, the system may repeatedly switch between Up and Down, paying spread and slippage every time.

A new signal must be strong enough to cover the cost of reducing the old position and building the new one.

## 6\. Why execution can matter more than the signal

Suppose the bot finds Up and Down available for a combined cost of 95¢ and submits both orders.

Up fills completely.

Before the Down order is filled, the book changes, sell orders disappear, and Down now costs 57¢.

The arbitrage no longer exists. But the bot is already holding open Up inventory. The failure did not come from the fair-value model. It came from incomplete execution.

The system now needs to decide:

> \> how long to wait for the second leg > how quickly to move its limit price > how much unhedged size is acceptable > when to remain a maker > when to use taker execution > when to close the first leg at a loss

An inventory-adjusted reservation price can be used to modify the bot’s quoting behavior.

**Reservation Price equals fair value minus inventory imbalance multiplied by risk aversion, volatility squared, and time remaining.**

If the bot already holds too much Up, the maximum price it is willing to pay for additional Up should fall.

At the same time, it may raise its bid for Down to reduce the imbalance more quickly.

The order type also affects execution:

> **GTC** remains active in the CLOB until it is filled or canceled.

> **GTD** automatically expires at a specified time.

> **FOK** must fill completely and immediately or the entire order is canceled.

> **FAK** takes the available size and cancels the unfilled remainder.

> **Post-only** ensures the order adds liquidity instead of immediately executing as a taker.

Most bots divide their intended size across several limit orders.

This allows them to:

> control their average fill price remove stale orders reduce size when the edge weakens avoid displaying their full intended position in one order

But passive execution is not always better.

When the market is repricing quickly, trying to save 1¢ with a maker order can cause the entire opportunity to disappear.

The Execution Engine must constantly balance price improvement against the probability of receiving a fill before the edge is gone.

## 7\. Risk management and backtesting determine how long the bot survives

Even a large net edge does not justify risking the entire bankroll.

Live trading always includes scenarios that cannot be completely eliminated:

> fair value may be wrong liquidity may disappear one side of the structure may not fill several markets may move against the bot simultaneously an external price feed may lag the API may return stale data

Fractional Kelly can be used as a starting point for position sizing.

**Fractional Kelly uses only a selected percentage of the full Kelly allocation, which is calculated from the probability of winning, the probability of losing, and the net payout relative to the capital at risk.**

```python
def fractional_kelly_size(
    win_probability,
    entry_price,
    kelly_fraction=0.25,
):
    if not 0 < entry_price < 1:
        raise ValueError(
            "Entry price must be between 0 and 1"
        )

    loss_probability = (
        1 - win_probability
    )

    net_odds = (
        1 - entry_price
    ) / entry_price

    full_kelly = (
        net_odds * win_probability
        - loss_probability
    ) / net_odds

    fractional_size = (
        full_kelly
        * kelly_fraction
    )

    return max(fractional_size, 0.0)

position_size = fractional_kelly_size(
    win_probability=0.60,
    entry_price=0.49,
    kelly_fraction=0.25,
)

print(
    f"Bankroll Allocation: "
    f"{position_size:.2%}"
)
```

Using 25% of full Kelly, for example, reduces the strategy’s dependence on any single probability estimate.

Hard limits are then applied:

> maximum size per market maximum exposure to BTC, ETH, or SOL maximum unhedged inventory daily loss limit maximum number of correlated positions automatic kill switch when data quality deteriorates

BTC Up or Down 5m and BTC Up or Down 15m may appear to be separate markets.

During a strong BTC move, however, both positions may represent the same directional exposure.

The same applies to ETH and SOL if the entire crypto market begins moving together.

Before live deployment, the strategy should be tested through backtesting or paper trading that includes:

> \> fees > realistic spread > slippage > partial fills > execution latency > canceled orders > maximum drawdown

A strategy that only makes money when every order fills at the best visible price is not ready for production.

**Why the same strategy performs differently for a human and a bot**

Even when a manual trader understands the market structure, several execution limitations remain:

> **Late entries** - the market may reprice before the trader confirms the move and submits the order. **Emotional sizing** - position size is often based on subjective confidence rather than expected value. **Fatigue** - speed and decision quality decline after hours of monitoring similar markets. **Drawdown reactions** - after a losing streak, a trader may abandon a valid strategy or increase risk to recover losses quickly.

Automation does not turn a bad model into a profitable one.

It simply applies the same sizing, execution, and shutdown rules during every trading cycle.

## 8\. What the complete Polymarket bot stack looks like

A production bot can usually be divided into six layers.

> **Layer 1 - Data**

External crypto prices, the resolution feed, CLOB order books, recent trades, active orders, and fills.

> **Layer 2 - Signals**

Price momentum, volatility, order book imbalance, trade flow, and deviations between related markets.

> **Layer 3 - Pricing**

Independent fair values for Up and Down based on the opening price and time remaining.

> **Layer 4 - Position Logic**

Temporal Arbitrage, Hedged Directional Position, Inventory Market Making, Near-Resolution Capture, or Dynamic Position Rotation.

> **Layer 5 - Execution and Risk**

Limit orders, incomplete-fill management, position sizing, exposure limits, and kill switches.

> **Layer 6 - Research**

Backtesting, paper trading, reconstruction of complete trading cycles, and analysis of unsuccessful executions.

Claude and other AI models can be used inside the research layer to:

> analyze historical trades identify recurring patterns write backtesting code group individual fills into positions investigate the causes of losing trades

But the live trading loop should operate according to predefined logic.

```python
async def run_polymarket_bot():
    while True:
        market_state = await receive_market_data()

        features = build_market_features(
            market_state
        )

        fair_values = estimate_fair_values(
            market_state,
            features,
        )

        opportunity = find_executable_edge(
            market_state,
            fair_values,
        )

        if opportunity is None:
            continue

        position_plan = choose_position_structure(
            market_state,
            opportunity,
        )

        order_plan = build_execution_plan(
            market_state,
            position_plan,
        )

        approved = risk_manager_approves(
            market_state,
            order_plan,
        )

        if approved:
            await submit_orders(order_plan)
```

In production, the system should not invent a new strategy after every market update.

Its job is much simpler:

receive data → calculate → check risk → submit orders
